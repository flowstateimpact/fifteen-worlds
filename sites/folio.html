Essay · 04

The river keeps the receipts

Every monsoon the Padma moves the way a debt moves: quietly, compounding, until one morning a family on the char finds the river has come to collect. The field they planted in March is water by September. The land that rises somewhere downstream belongs, for now, to nobody.

Hydrologists call this erosion and accretion. The farmers on the chars call it the river's accounting. It takes a field here and writes one there, and it never loses track of a single grain.

I spent eleven days on a launch between Barishal and Sadarghat trying to learn who reads that ledger. The answer, it turned out, was almost everyone except the people who draw the maps.

Reportage · 19

The launch captain has made the same crossing for twenty-six years and keeps no charts. He keeps a notebook. Each page is a date and a list of what has changed: a sandbar off Chandpur grown longer, a ghat washed away, a new channel cut behind an island that did not exist when his son was born.

He showed me the government survey sheet folded in the wheelhouse drawer. It was eleven years old. He tapped it twice and said the line that became this issue's pull quote.

He is right in a way that should worry anyone who builds near water. A map assumes the ground will wait. The delta has never agreed to that.

Bangladesh sits on the largest river delta on earth, built by the Ganges, the Brahmaputra and the Meghna.

Fiction · 41

Ferry, 5:40 am

The horn goes twice before anyone is ready. My mother is still tying the last knot on the tiffin carrier, and my brother is asleep on the sacks of rice as if he paid extra for them. On the upper deck a man sells tea from a kettle that has been hot, I think, since before I was born.

We are going to Dhaka because my father is there. Nobody has said it any other way.

The river is the colour of milk tea. When the engine starts the whole boat shivers, and so do I, and my mother puts her hand on the back of my neck without looking, the way she does when she is counting us.
